The story in one minute
  • Hasnain treated the lesson as a mono grade class. Symmetry suited both grades, so he taught Grades 3 and 4 together with no rotation. About 40% of the lesson came from our plan, 30% from his own side, the rest from AI.
  • Both teachers already rotate, without the name. When two grades' topics are too different, they teach one grade, set work, move to the other and come back. Our version of rotation is harder for them to run.
  • The content works. The structure needs to fit their day. Our plan runs 45 minutes and their periods run 30 to 40. A shorter plan would be easier to read and use.
  • They suggested one book for a combined class. Grades 1 and 2, and Grades 3 and 4, share mostly the same topics.
  • Hasnain's school joined the multigrade pilot late, so the plan reached him as a forward and he had not seen the video.
  • Next: help teachers understand what multigrade is and why it needs a different strategy, and take plan length to the content team. Other feature feedback is at the end.

10:45 · Grades 3 and 4 · Maths

Symmetry for two grades, taught as one class

We observed Hasnain Iftikhar teach Grades 3 and 4 Maths together, as one mono grade class with no rotation. The lesson was Symmetry in Shapes, from Unit 2 (Number Operations) and Unit 6 (Geometry). The theme was the same for both grades. The SLOs differ slightly.

Grade 3

Identify the number of lines of symmetry in 2D shapes and write the answer.

Textbook pp. 161 to 165 · our plan uses square, isosceles triangle, rectangle

Grade 4

Determine symmetry and reflection for given shapes and record the number of symmetry lines.

Textbook pp. 159 to 160 · our plan uses kite, regular pentagon, parallelogram, rhombus

Our plan calls the Grade 4 idea reflection. Hasnain named it mirror image, and taught it his own way.

The first thing I noticed was the seating. Grade 3 and Grade 4 children sat mixed, one of each on every bench. When I asked why, he said he pairs a weak learner with a strong learner from the other grade, in both directions.
  1. 10:45 · The hookHe drew shapes, a triangle and a square, on the board and asked how many corners each has. Almost the whole class answered together. A few stayed quiet and were smiling, so I think they were shy rather than lost.
  2. Next · The explanationHe explained the concept to both grades together on the board.
  3. Then · Paper foldingHe used printed paper shapes, folding them to show lines of symmetry and mirror image, and kept asking whether a shape stays the same when divided in half.
  4. 10:50 · A recap for Grade 3He felt the hook alone was not enough for them. They seemed to have forgotten shapes like the hexagon and pentagon, so he took them through the shapes from one to eight.
  5. Group activityMixed groups of four, one printed shape per group. One child identified the shape, one said whether it was symmetrical, one gave the number of symmetry lines (or why there were none), and one presented.
  6. PresentationsOne or two groups presented, standing or sitting at their own places, and he corrected a few.
  7. 11:15 · HomeworkThe class ended with homework. In total it ran about 30 minutes.

What he adapted

  • The rotation. Our plan has one teacher with one grade at a time: Grade 3 for 18 minutes while Grade 4 works alone, then Grade 4 for 18 minutes while Grade 3 works alone. He taught both grades together.
  • The prior knowledge. He added a recap with Grade 3 at 10:50, because the hook was not enough for them in his judgment.
  • The individual You Do, where the teacher walks to each grade and checks three new questions per child. Homework took its place.
  • The wrap up and exit ticket, which a 30 minute period had no room for.

What I saw working

  • The plan's content was used well and fitted to the class.
  • Peer teaching across grades, which he does on instinct. This is exactly what multigrade teaching builds on.
  • Mixed seating by design.
  • Clear roles in every group.
  • Prepared printed fold sheets.
  • Checking understanding with students throughout.
  • A teacher who noticed that Grade 3 needed more prior knowledge and fixed it in the moment.

Gaps I noticed

  • No rotation. The theme was shared and the SLO difference was small, so this fits their own rule. But Grade 4 had an extra SLO (reflection) that the lesson covered with both grades together.
  • Reflection was taught to both grades, but our plan gives it to Grade 4 only. Grade 3 is examined on their own book.
  • No individual You Do.
  • The plan is longer than the period.
11:15 · Habib and Hasnain, after the class

Two ways to teach two grades, and both teachers already rotate

After the class ended we sat down again with Habib and Hasnain. I asked what they thought of our lesson plan and how rotation worked for them. They found the rotation strategy we gave them hard to run in their classroom.

Because I had just watched the lesson, my first reading was that they treat two grades as one mono grade class. When I asked how they would teach two grades instead, they said more staff would help more than more strategies. We told them plainly that staffing is not something we control.

So I asked what steps a multigrade lesson plan should carry. They are aware of the gradual release of responsibility (GRR) model. Their answer: SLO, a hook to engage, an explanation with some technique, independent practice and guided practice (or one of these), and homework. Then Habib explained how he manages two grades. This is the key finding of the visit.

Habib prefers this

Same topic for both grades

He explains once, to everyone.

Topics too different

One grade needs focus

  1. Teach the smaller grade first, for example Grade 3.
  2. Give them work. He knows they will not work on their own and will lose focus, but he has to.
  3. Move to Grade 4 and teach them.
  4. Come back.

That second method is rotation. Both teachers use it, without knowing the name.

Habib does not prefer it, because every time he moves to the other grade he loses track of what he taught the first. Hasnain agreed that he also uses it, on the same basis.

This explains the lesson we saw. The two SLOs were close enough that Hasnain judged they could be taught together, so he did not rotate. Our plan rotates even for close SLOs, so for this kind of lesson it offers more structure than they felt was needed.

Our plan is 45 minutes. Their periods are 30 to 40.

Their feedback on our plan

  • Their first thought on opening it was that it is long to read.
  • Rotation is the most demanding part. Children and teacher both find it hard to hold concentration with the back and forth.
  • They understand the rotation map clearly, but the time division does not yet match their periods. One said, lightly, that the map looked like a lot of time for what it covers.
  • They asked for at least four to five periods of about an hour each when two or more grades must learn something.

What they need is a plan that is quicker to read and a time division that fits the period they have.

Books and chunking sheets

They suggested merging the Grade 1 and 2 books, and the Grade 3 and 4 books, because they think the topics are mostly the same. For a multigrade class there should be one book.

That is not in our control, but we have already aligned similar topics and SLOs. I showed them our Urdu and Maths chunking sheets, which align Grade 1 to 5 SLOs by skill or type. Both teachers were happy with them. They said we are making sure they are satisfied with our product, and I told them that is literally my job.

Access · how the plan arrived

The plan reached Hasnain as a forward

Hasnain's school was not in the original 46 multigrade pilot schools in Rawalpindi. It was added later, because one of the 46 has become a mono school: they hired two more outsourced teachers, making five, so we replaced it with this school.

That is why Hasnain could not generate a multigrade plan on his own. We will enable it. Until then the plan reached him as a forward on his phone, and he had not watched the video tutorial, so he did not know what the feature is, how it works, or why rotation is part of it. After I sent him the workflow video he said he understood it.

What Rumi's records add. No symmetry plan sits in Hasnain's account. His latest plan is from 16 September and his latest AI-coach recording is from 6 October, with nothing from 7 October as of 8 October. That fits a plan that did not come through his own account.
After

What I am taking from this visit

Many Rawalpindi teachers already do some form of rotation without knowing it has a name. Others are new to multigrade and manage it as mono grade, because nobody has shown them the difference. Most teachers also told us they are new to multigrade and need time to adapt. Habib and Hasnain said the same, so we need to give them more time.

Most Rawalpindi teachers are not aware of what multigrade is. They think a class is multigrade if one room carries two grades with two teachers teaching them separately in the same space, or if one teacher teaches both together as Hasnain did, or if a helper handles the class that needs less focus. That is why version 2 of the onboarding video has a section on the difference between mono grade and multigrade. The video has gone out to teachers, and we are calling every school to confirm.

Two things change how we should position the plan.

  • We should not introduce rotation as a new strategy. We should show teachers that what they already do when topics differ is rotation, and give them a structure that makes it easier and shorter.
  • Teachers use our content even when they change our structure. The content is working. The structure is what needs to fit their reality.

The teachers were happy to be part of the pilot. They will keep using multigrade lesson plans and share feedback. Digital coaching is new to multigrade as well, so I explained how it works in a multigrade classroom. The video covers it, and they will upload their lessons.

Next steps with teachers

  1. Enable the multigrade feature for Hasnain's school, so plans reach him through his own account.
  2. Help teachers understand what a multigrade classroom is, how it differs from a mono grade class, and that it needs a different teaching strategy such as rotation. Walk Habib and Hasnain through the video from there.
  3. Plan an onboarding session that demos how to deliver a multigrade lesson plan, framed as "you already do this, here is how to do it with less effort." Habib and Hasnain asked for exactly this: an all-teachers session, or someone who comes to the school and delivers a plan in front of them.
  4. Give teachers more time to adapt, and collect data from a few more schools. They told us they need it.
  5. Reinforce that in a combined class each grade works to its own SLO and its own book.

Next steps with the content team

  1. Raise plan length. Our plan is 45 minutes and school periods are usually 30 to 40. We need a shorter version or clear guidance on what to cut.
  2. Ask whether plans with close SLOs, like this one, should offer a combined delivery option, since Hasnain merged them on his own. Take the teachers' suggestion of one book for a combined class with it.
More feature feedback

The exam generator and At Home Learning

Two other features came up. They are separate from multigrade.

Exam generator

I asked them to generate a worksheet and they did it on their own, with their own numbers. Today they write papers by hand and then print them. Now they can generate a PDF and send it straight to printing. They said we have solved some of their problems.

Two pieces of feedback: fit more questions on one page, with a smaller font or a different alignment, so printing costs less. And Grades 1 and 2 need pictures, for questions like "look at the picture and tell what action is happening" or "what noun is this."

Next step 8: collect more feedback on page density and pictures for Grades 1 and 2, then decide what to change.

At Home Learning (AHL)

I explained the video and quiz feature. They did not understand it at first, but they do now and will send it to students.

The problem: only about 40% of their students have a smartphone at home. They asked whether we can help them run the video and quiz inside the class so every child can take part. For that, they requested an LED for the classroom.

Next step 9: collect more feedback on the smartphone gap and in-class use with a classroom LED, then decide what to do.
